\begin{abstract}
While existing EV charging datasets and protocols provide general facility locations, they suffer from fragmented schemas, unstructured temporal data, and a lack of vehicle-specific operational constraints. Consequently, determining whether a specific vehicle can practically utilize a charging station remains a manual hurdle for drivers. This paper introduces \textbf{PluggedIn}, an integrated semantic data model and knowledge graph architecture designed to bridge public data silos with industry-grade structuring. By formalizing adapter-mediated compatibility, multi-network driver memberships, structured operating periods, and granular organizational roles, PluggedIn enables machine-interpretable, vehicle-specific accessibility queries. The complete project repository, data models, and documentation are available at \url{github.com/chohoon901/PluggedIn}.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{Electric Vehicles, Knowledge Graph, Charging Accessibility, Data Modeling, Semantic Interoperability}

\section{Introduction}
% ===============================================================
For widespread electric vehicle adoption, drivers must be able to charge reliably when and where needed. For drivers operating on fixed schedules, locating a charging station is only the first step; practical usability depends on a complex intersection of factors including connector compatibility, operating hours, access restrictions, payment network memberships, and vehicle-specific suitability~\cite{qi2023evkg}. 

Currently, public charging data is scattered across heterogeneous sources with fragmented schemas and ambiguous free-text fields~\cite{liu2025evca}, while robust organizational standards remain locked within closed, B2B protocols. As a result, critical conditions, such as adapter-mediated compatibility or active network payment authorization, are routinely left to manual judgment. This project introduces \textbf{PluggedIn}, an integrated semantic data model and knowledge graph designed to elevate open public data to industry-standard structural rigor, enabling machine-interpretable, vehicle-specific accessibility queries.

\section{History}
% ===============================================================
The tracking and representation of energy distribution and refueling infrastructure have evolved significantly, providing an essential context for modern electric vehicle charging models. Long before computer networks, tracking fuel relied entirely on analog methods, such as paper maps and physical ledgers, requiring manual coordination. Later, the rise of web applications introduced Application Programming Interfaces, such as the Alternative Fuels Data Center (AFDC), and aggregators for dynamic location lookups~\cite{afdc_web}. However, these independent platforms suffered from fragmented schemas and unstructured fields, ultimately driving the modern adoption of semantic knowledge graphs to integrate diverse vehicle and infrastructure constraints.

\subsection{Hierarchy of Existing Representations}
Existing EV charging information representations take different forms and structure information according to different purposes and modeling perspectives. We classify the examined representations into three categories: (1) Charging Infrastructure Data Representations, (2) Interoperability Protocols, and (3) Semantic Knowledge Representations.

Charging Infrastructure Data Representations, including the National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR) Alternative Fuels Data Center (AFDC) API, Open Charge Map (OCM), and OpenStreetMap (OSM), primarily aim to record and provide information about real-world charging infrastructure. NLR and OCM provide structured charging-facility records through APIs~\cite{nlr,ocm}, whereas OSM represents charging facilities as geographic features described by tags~\cite{osm}.

The second category, Interoperability Protocols, includes the Open Charge Point Interface (OCPI) and Open InterCharge Protocol (OICP). Both standardize information exchange among charging actors and systems: OCPI supports information exchange among actors such as CPOs and eMSPs~\cite{ocpi}, while OICP supports eRoaming and related exchange between CPOs and EMPs~\cite{oicp}. Although both protocols also provide detailed representations of charging infrastructure, unlike NLR, OCM, and OSM, their primary purpose is to support standardized information exchange and interoperability among different systems and service providers.

A different perspective is taken by Semantic Knowledge Representations, which focus on connecting EV-related information through semantic concepts and relationships. EVKG connects EVs, charging infrastructure, connectors, and their relationships through an ontology and knowledge graph~\cite{evkg}, while the Urban EV Charging Activity (EVCA) ontology integrates heterogeneous EV charging data semantically~\cite{evca}. Overall, this classification shows that EV charging information has been modeled for different purposes and from different modeling perspectives: representing real-world infrastructure data, enabling information exchange between systems, and providing semantic knowledge representations.

The NLR dataset is the official list of alternative-fuel stations operated by the U.S. Department of Energy~\cite{nlr}. It stores each station as a single record, with the network as a single string field and operating hours as free text. As a result, it is difficult to determine whether a station is open at a given time, which account can be used for payment, or whether a port fits the vehicle. Its encoding of access restrictions and vehicle class as codes can be a reference for our project.

Open Charge Map (OCM) is a crowdsourced charging station platform~\cite{ocm}. It records the type, power, and quantity of each connector separately under each station (POI), which shows that about 20\% of BC stations have two or more connector types.\footnote{From our analysis of BC extracts retrieved on 5~October~2026: 2,646 OCM stations (OCM API) and 885 OSM stations (Overpass API).} However, operating hours are mixed into free text, organizations are represented by a single operator field, and 83\% of BC records come from the NLR dataset, so they inherit its limitations. Its separation of connectors from stations is a direct reference for our project.

OpenStreetMap (OSM) provides keys for owner, operator, network, operating hours, and truck access~\cite{osm}, but none of them is enforced; in BC, the owner key was never used, and the network, connector, and operating-hours keys were filled for 22\% or less~\cite{osm_data}. It shows the gap between expressible structure and actual data.

OCPI is a roaming protocol between businesses that structures day-of-week operating hours, permitted parking vehicle types, and owners and operators within a location--EVSE--connector hierarchy~\cite{ocpi}, and it comes closest to answering our questions. However, this data is exchanged only between contracted businesses and is not public. Its three-level hierarchy and day-of-week hours can be a reference for our project.

EVKG is a policy-oriented knowledge graph that links electric vehicle adoption, charging infrastructure, and the power grid in the United States~\cite{evkg}. By representing direct vehicle--connector compatibility, it serves as the starting point for our model. However, it does not represent adapter-mediated compatibility or a driver's network membership, and its operating hours are strings that are not machine-readable.

EVCA is an urban-planning ontology that links charging station, neighbourhood, and travel data in Eindhoven~\cite{evca}. It is designed for aggregations such as the number of stations per neighbourhood, and because it has no operating hours, access conditions, or vehicle compatibility, it cannot answer whether an individual station can be used now. Its conversion of OCM data into RDF can guide later stages of our project.

\section{Entity-Relationship Diagram and Project Positioning}
Existing models record where charging stations are (AFDC, OCM, OSM), support planning and analysis (EVKG, EVCA), or exchange operational data privately between businesses (OCPI), but none of the public models is organized around whether a particular driver can actually use a particular station. Even EVKG, which demonstrates a personalized station search, fixes the driver's conditions in the query rather than representing them in the model. Therefore, we built our model around a single usability question: can this vehicle, driven by this driver and arriving at this time, charge at this station? Answering it requires checking whether the station is open, whether the vehicle is allowed to enter, whether its inlet can receive one of the station's plugs, and whether the driver can pay. Modelling the domain from the driver and vehicle's side in this way let us find more data which is more available for users.

First, none of the six models above shows which adapter is compatible with which connector. In North America, CCS1 and NACS (Tesla) are currently in use together, so a vehicle can often still charge with an adapter even when the charger's plug and the vehicle's inlet have different shapes. In existing data, however, this judgement is left to the driver; in more than 20 BC records in OCM~\cite{ocm}, the notice ``check Tesla's online station map to see if an adapter is required'' remains only as free text, and EVKG represents only direct compatibility~\cite{evkg}. We therefore created an \texttt{Adapter} entity linked through \texttt{ConnectorType} to \texttt{Port} and \texttt{Vehicle}. Because an adapter works in only one direction, we separated the charger-side and vehicle-side shapes into two relationships (\texttt{ConvertsFrom} and \texttt{ConvertsTo}), and its \texttt{MaxPowerKW} shows that it may limit charging speed.

Second, none of the models records whether a driver has joined a particular network. Existing data tells only which network a station belongs to, and whether the driver can therefore pay has to be judged by the driver. EVKG specifies the network directly in the query~\cite{evkg}, so a driver's network memberships are not stored in the graph. By connecting \texttt{Driver} to \texttt{Network} (\texttt{MemberOf}), we made it possible to answer the question ``Can I pay at that station?'', and for drivers who have not joined any network, the station's \texttt{AcceptsGuestPayment} attribute is used instead.

We also found other shortcomings. Most of the models above store operating hours as free text, and values that are not operating hours are sometimes mixed in, so they cannot be read by a machine. For this reason, we separated \texttt{OperatingPeriod} from \texttt{Station}, so that stations that operate only at specific times, where a driver is more likely to make a wasted trip (77 stations in BC, of which 45 open only on weekdays and 7 only at night), can be used in queries. In addition, most models use a single field for a station's owner, operator, and network. We divided the relationship between a station and an organization into \texttt{OwnedBy}, \texttt{OperatedBy}, and \texttt{ServedBy}, and applied an ISA structure to \texttt{Organization}, so that an organization is stored only once even when it plays several roles.

Since the final goal of this project is a knowledge graph, we designed the ERD with it in mind. We made \texttt{ConnectorType} an entity so that differently named connectors from different sources can share one \texttt{ConnectorType}, and we named relationships as verbs so that they can be used as the predicates of triples. For relationships with attributes, such as \texttt{MemberOf}, we plan to place \texttt{Membership} as a separate node. Finally, this ERD is a draft, and roaming between networks and highway corridors will be added later.
